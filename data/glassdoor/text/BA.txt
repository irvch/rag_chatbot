	Pros: 
		The benefits are the standout feature to me – healthcare insurance and the 401k are very good. Also in the area of compensation, they pay for overtime, which isn’t typical for engineering jobs. 
		
		Opportunities to move around within the company can be a major plus and help you get promotions and bigger raises faster. Whether it’s projects, roles, or locations, there’s a lot of room to explore.
		
		I believe the compensation is fair for the broader engineering market. However I do question how competitive it is for the industry: I passed up an offer from a competitor that would have raised my pay by nearly 50%. I turned it down because, based on my experience at Boeing (especially the last year) I had no desire to stay in big aero no matter how much the pay.


	Cons: 
		If you're all about tradition, love following rules, and like to settle into a routine where you’re told what to do and how to do it, you might just fit in perfectly here. It’s all about sticking to the script here. It's like working on the production line in an engineering factory.
		
		But if you’re the type who likes to color outside the lines, thrives under creative chaos, and loves to innovate, think again. You’ll likely find yourself butting heads with the status quo and the ‘lifers’, especially the managers who aren't typically fans of shaking things up. It’s like being a square peg in a round hole, and let’s just say not everyone’s going to be thrilled about your groundbreaking ideas.
		
		Dreaming of cutting-edge aerospace work? You might need to adjust your expectations. Aerospace is incredible, but it’s also very cautious by nature and tends to move at a glacial pace when it comes to innovation. You’ll likely spend more time fine-tuning the old than inventing the new.
		
		And about those high-tech tools you were looking forward to? Think more vintage than cutting edge. Upgrading software can be like moving mountains, so don’t hold your breath unless the software is about to become unsupported by the manufacturer. It was a real eye-opener for me when I switched jobs and saw what modern engineering tools could do. It’s way beyond most structural analysis done at Boeing in its sophistication. There’s a whole world of tech out there that makes some of the old stuff still used at Boeing look like child’s play.
		
		Seeing new grad engineers get reduced to spreadsheet jockeys from day one was tough. I kept wanting to tell them, "You sure this is the dream job you were after?" It’s not just about the tools, though. It’s about getting to do real, hands-on engineering, something that’s pretty rare in big aero. Feeling like you’re tethered to a computer all day, every day, and never touching anything real gets old fast for some. Also, at other companies I’ve found a freedom to pursue new ideas I’m interested in, along with an appreciation of my drive for innovation. At Boeing, I found such ideas are typically not appreciated and normally get shut down: “We don’t have the budget”, “we don’t have the staffing”, “that’s not within our scope”.
		
		Sticking around for the steady paycheck and benefits is easy, but don’t let it turn into a trap. Some call it the ‘golden handcuffs’—comfortable, yet limiting. Keep in mind, unless you’re looking to make a career of big aero, working at Boeing probably won’t make you very marketable for the vast majority of employers, most of which don’t have such hyper-specialization in their engineering roles.
		
		Some might consider getting a role on a Phantom Works project to be the dream. Just keep in mind even PW isn’t all about groundbreaking work; most of it is heavily derivative in nature. For me, earning a few patents on a PW project was a highlight, showing that it’s not impossible to innovate.
		
		Looking back at my time with Boeing, it simply didn’t have the fun or happy atmosphere I've found elsewhere. A big reason, I think, is that a lot of people didn't seem all that excited about the work. And can you blame them? When you've been doing the same relatively narrow tasks day in, day out, for years—or even decades—it's no surprise when the job loses its shine. When the intrinsic rewards of the work don’t cut it anymore, people tend to get obsessed with extrinsic rewards, like raises, promotions, bonuses, or any perks that come their way (yes at Boeing there was an obsession compared to other places I’ve worked). The thing is, big extrinsic rewards like promotions and raises are few and far between, and their luster fades quickly as they become the ‘new normal’ .
		
		Finding my way to a job where my knack for innovation is actually celebrated was a game-changer. Now I feel like a valued, trusted, and important part of the whole; not just one of thousands of anonymous units of engineering labor. Also, I feel like a real salaried employee who has agency and is not constantly being ‘watched’. This is a stark contrast to Boeing where I felt salaried in classification only, and had to charge time to the 1/10th of an hour. It’s a reminder that there’s a perfect fit out there for everyone—you just gotta keep looking.
		
		Thinking about the future, with all the hype about AI and automation, there’s lots of potential for shaking things up in aerospace. There’s a lot of moving data from one place to another and doing standard work according to well-established rules and procedures, and we all know those types of things are ripe for disruption by automation. Just something to think about.
		Even though this cons section is much longer than the pros section, I’m not trying to put down my old employer. However I do feel I owe prospective employees an honest assessment. Even though I stayed far too long, it was a chapter in my life that set me up for where I am now, working my dream job. Also, this is for you unconventional types who might be feeling a bit out of place wherever you are, wondering if there’s more to engineering than landing a job at a big company. I hope you find that place where your brilliance shines.


	Summary: 
		Might be ok for those who color within the lines
